# Hypokalemia

## Basics

- Hypokalemia: serum K <3.5 mEq/L; severe <2.5 mEq/L (Grams et al., JAMA, 2021)
- Pseudohypokalemia (rare): delayed specimen processing; K uptake by leukemic cells when blood with a very high WBC count sits at room temperature (Gennari, New England Journal of Medicine, 1998)
- Total-body K ≈ 3,500 mEq (Allegretti et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2024)
    - 98% intracellular (mainly skeletal muscle)
    - 2% extracellular (~60 mEq)



## Renal K handling

- Filtered K is largely reabsorbed in the proximal tubule and loop of Henle; only ~10% of the filtered load reaches the distal nephron
- Excretion is set by the distal nephron (connecting tubule, cortical collecting duct) (Gumz et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2015):
    - Principal cells: Na enters via ENaC --> lumen-negative potential --> K secreted via ROMK
    - Intercalated cells: H-K-ATPase reabsorbs K (upregulated in K depletion)
- Two main regulators of K secretion: distal Na delivery and aldosterone (Grams et al., JAMA, 2021)

Coupling of distal Na delivery and aldosterone (Palmer, American Journal of Kidney Diseases, 2010):

- High distal Na --> more Na entry via ENaC --> more K loss via ROMK
- High aldosterone --> more ENaC activity --> more Na entry, more K (and H+) loss
- Normally the two are inversely coupled:
    - Volume expansion --> high distal Na delivery, low RAAS/aldosterone
    - Volume depletion --> low distal Na delivery, RAAS activation
- Hence volume status has minimal effect on K excretion, which tracks dietary intake
- Renal K wasting occurs when the two become directly coupled (both high)

## Approach

1. Exclude pseudohypokalemia; review drugs, vomiting/diarrhea history
2. Urine K: renal vs extrarenal loss/shift
    - 24-hr urine K <20 mEq (or spot urine K <15 mEq/L) --> extrarenal loss or shift (Allegretti et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2024)
    - Spot urine K/Cr >22 mEq/g (≈2.5 mmol/mmol) --> renal loss (Grams et al., JAMA, 2021; Lin et al., Archives of Internal Medicine, 2004)
    - TTKG is no longer recommended (Grams et al., JAMA, 2021)
    - Kidney can lower K excretion to ~15–20 mEq/day, so low intake alone rarely causes hypokalemia
3. If renal loss: BP/volume status --> acid-base status --> urine Cl --> renin/aldosterone
4. Always check Mg

## Low intake

- Anorexia nervosa, crash diets, alcoholism, intestinal malabsorption
- Rarely sufficient alone; usually worsens another cause

## Intracellular shift

Physiological shifts:

- Postprandial insulin: moves ingested K into cells
- Exercise-released catecholamines: move K released from muscle back into cells

Causes:

- Alkalosis (effect is trivial)
- Insulin administration, refeeding
- β2-adrenergic stimulation
    - Stress-induced epinephrine release
    - Drugs: albuterol, terbutaline, ritodrine, theophylline intoxication, caffeine, clenbuterol (body builders, adulterated heroin)
- Anabolism
    - Treatment of pernicious anemia
    - Rapidly growing leukemias and lymphomas
- Hypokalemic periodic paralysis: precipitated by rest after exercise, stress, high-carbohydrate meals (catecholamine or insulin surges)
    - Thyrotoxic (acquired): mainly Asian and Hispanic men
    - Familial: AD, onset <20 yr, skeletal muscle Ca or Na channel mutations
    - Total-body K is normal --> replace K cautiously (rebound hyperkalemia) (Lin et al., Archives of Internal Medicine, 2004)
- Hypothermia
- Toxins: barium, chloroquine, cesium salts (herbal "antitumor" preparations)

## Extrarenal loss

- Excessive sweating
- GI: diarrhea, laxatives
    - Non-AG metabolic acidosis; acidosis shifts K out of cells and can mask the total-body deficit
    - Volume depletion --> secondary hyperaldosteronism adds some renal K loss

## Renal loss

### Mg deficiency

- Intracellular Mg normally blocks K secretion through ROMK; low Mg releases this block
- Usually also needs increased distal Na delivery or high aldosterone to cause hypokalemia (Huang and Kuo, Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, 2007)
- Hypokalemia is refractory until Mg is repleted (Touyz et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2024)

### Primary increase in mineralocorticoid activity

- Na/water retention --> ECF expansion, HTN, metabolic alkalosis; high distal Na delivery + high mineralocorticoid effect
- Usually no edema because of aldosterone escape: after a few kg of Na/water retention, ANP and pressure natriuresis restore Na excretion
- High dietary Na worsens K loss (more distal Na delivery)

Causes by renin/aldosterone pattern:

- High renin / high aldosterone (primary increase in renin)
    - Renal artery stenosis
    - Reninoma

- Low renin / high aldosterone (primary aldosteronism)
    - Bilateral adrenal hyperplasia (~2/3) (Rafey et al., JAMA, 2026)
    - Aldosterone-producing adenoma, Conn syndrome (~1/3)
    - Glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism (familial hyperaldosteronism type I): chimeric CYP11B1/CYP11B2 gene puts aldosterone synthase under ACTH control --> suppressed by dexamethasone (Vaidya et al., Nature Reviews Disease Primers, 2026)
    - Note: most patients with primary aldosteronism are normokalemic

- Low renin / low aldosterone (nonaldosterone mineralocorticoid)
    - Cushing syndrome (especially ectopic ACTH): very high cortisol overwhelms 11β-HSD2 and binds MR
		    ![391](../../Attachments/hypokalemia-cortisol.png)
    - 11β-hydroxylase deficiency: 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC) accumulates and activates MR; HTN + virilization
    - 17α-hydroxylase deficiency: DOC excess; HTN + sexual infantilism
		    ![523](../../Attachments/hypokalemia-adrenal-steroidogenesis.png)
    - Apparent mineralocorticoid excess: 11β-HSD2 deficiency (AR) lets normal cortisol reach MR; licorice is the acquired form (blocks the same enzyme)

- Low renin / low aldosterone (mineralocorticoid-like effect) (Borozan et al., Current Hypertension Reports, 2025)
    - Liddle syndrome: ENaC gain-of-function, AD; no response to MRAs; responds to amiloride or triamterene
    - Geller syndrome: activating MR mutation, AD; progesterone activates MR (HTN worsens in pregnancy); spironolactone worsens HTN

### Primary increase in distal Na delivery

- Normal/low ECF volume --> secondary aldosterone increase; BP normal or low
- Causes:
    - Diuretics; Bartter and Gitelman syndromes
    - Nonreabsorbable anions that drag Na distally and make the lumen more negative: bicarbonate (vomiting, proximal RTA), ketones, Na salts of penicillins
- Differentiate by acid-base status:
    - Metabolic acidosis
        - Proximal RTA: bicarbonaturia --> distal Na delivery + volume loss/RAAS activation; oral bicarbonate worsens K wasting
        - Distal RTA: acidosis reduces proximal Na reabsorption; defective H-K-ATPase reduces distal K reabsorption; amphotericin causes K leak
        - DKA: ketoanions + osmotic diuresis
    - Metabolic alkalosis
        - Vomiting/NG suction: K loss is mainly renal (bicarbonaturia + secondary hyperaldosteronism); urine Cl low
        - Loop diuretics / Bartter syndrome: more severe, impaired urine concentration, hypercalciuria; urine Cl high
        - Thiazides / Gitelman syndrome: milder, intact concentrating ability, hypomagnesemia, hypocalciuria; urine Cl high (Urwin et al., Clinical Endocrinology, 2020)
        - Surreptitious vomiting or diuretic use mimics the tubulopathies; urine Na/Cl and a diuretic screen help (Grams et al., JAMA, 2021)

## Manifestations

- Low extracellular K --> hyperpolarized cell membrane --> less excitable
- Muscle: weakness, ascending paralysis, respiratory failure; myopathy and [Acute-Kidney-Injury/Rhabdomyolysis](Acute-Kidney-Injury/Rhabdomyolysis) (usually K <2.5 mEq/L) (Brown and Paloian, Pediatrics in Review, 2023)
- GI: ileus, constipation
- Cardiac: ST depression, flat T, prominent U, long PR/QT; atrial and ventricular arrhythmias
    - Risk is highest with ischemia, heart failure, LVH, or digoxin (Gennari, New England Journal of Medicine, 1998)
- CNS: confusion
- Renal:
    - ADH resistance --> polyuria, polydipsia
    - Kaliopenic nephropathy: chronic tubulointerstitial disease with polyuria, proteinuria, renal cysts, loss of kidney function
- Metabolic: less insulin release --> glucose intolerance
    - Thiazide trials: fall in K correlates with rise in glucose (Zillich et al., Hypertension, 2006); roughly 10 mg/dL per 1 mEq/L fall in K (approximate, thiazide data only)
- Raises BP when Na intake is not restricted (renal Na retention) (Gennari, New England Journal of Medicine, 1998)
